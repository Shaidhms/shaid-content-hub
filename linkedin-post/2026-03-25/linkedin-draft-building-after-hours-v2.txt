Would you take a 50% pay cut to work on something you actually care about?





I've been thinking about this a lot lately.

I spent 15 years building a career in performance engineering.
Good companies. International experience. Stable income.
On paper, everything looked right.

But something shifted when I started building with AI.
Not at work. After work.
Late nights. Weekends. Stolen hours between meetings.

And I realized something uncomfortable:

I was more alive at 11 PM building my own thing
than I was at 11 AM doing what I was paid for.

That's not a motivation problem.
That's a misalignment problem.

Here's what I've noticed about career decisions in the AI era:

The people who are thriving right now aren't the ones
with the best titles or the highest salaries.
They're the ones who positioned themselves
where their curiosity and the market overlap.

Performance engineering taught me systems thinking.
How things break under pressure. How to design for scale.
That didn't become irrelevant when I moved toward AI.
It became my unfair advantage.

Every skill you've built is transferable.
But only if you're willing to reframe it.

The real career question in 2026 isn't
"What job should I apply for?"

It's this:

. Where does my experience solve a problem the market hasn't fully recognized yet?
. Am I optimizing for salary or for leverage?
. What would I build if money wasn't the constraint?
. Is my current role funding my future or just funding my present?
. Am I learning something new every quarter or recycling what I already know?

AI didn't replace my career.
It forced me to rethink what my career is actually for.

A job is income.
A career is positioning.
A calling is when those two stop fighting each other.

Most people change jobs.
Few people change trajectories.

The ones who do don't wait for permission.
They start building proof.

If your 9-to-5 feels like a ceiling,
maybe it's not the job that needs changing.

Maybe it's the question you're asking about it.
